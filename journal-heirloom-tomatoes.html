Why we pay more for an ugly tomato

By Marisol Vandergriff, executive chef July 14, 2026 9 minute read

Every August I have the same conversation with somebody at a table. They ask why the tomato plate costs sixteen dollars when tomatoes are three dollars a pound at the supermarket in the same week. It is a fair question and it deserves a real answer rather than the word "local" said twice.

The short version is that we buy fruit the supermarket cannot legally sell at full price, and we pay a premium for it. That sounds backwards. Here is why it is not.

Grading was invented to solve a shipping problem

The United States Department of Agriculture publishes voluntary grade standards for fresh tomatoes. They cover things like shape, colour uniformity, freedom from scars, cracks, catfacing and sunscald, and a size range measured across the widest point. A fruit that meets the top grade is round, unblemished, firm and uniform. Nowhere in those standards is there a line about flavor, because flavor cannot be measured with a caliper on a loading dock at four in the morning.

This is not a conspiracy. Those standards exist for a genuine reason. If you are moving a truckload of tomatoes fifteen hundred miles, you need fruit that will survive the trip, stack without bruising, and arrive looking identical to the fruit in the photo on the shelf tag. Firmness and uniformity are exactly the right things to optimise for when the fruit has to travel. The problem is that everything you breed for firmness, you tend to breed away from taste. Thicker cell walls make a tomato that ships beautifully and eats like a wet tennis ball.

So the grading system quietly became a breeding brief. Growers who sell into wholesale get paid on grade, so they plant varieties that grade well. Over decades, that pushed the commercial tomato toward a fruit that is round, red, firm, uniform and largely without character.

What falls out the bottom

Now think about what happens to fruit that fails. A tomato with a healed crack across the shoulder, a lobed and creased Brandywine, a green-shouldered Cherokee Purple that never turns fully red because it is not supposed to, a fruit that came in at four inches when the pack demanded three: all of that is off-grade. It gets sold as processing fruit at a fraction of the price, or it stays in the field.

The unpleasant arithmetic is that a small farm growing genuinely good heirloom varieties will lose a large share of the crop to cosmetic grading. On the six farms inside our radius that grow tomatoes seriously, off-grade runs between a quarter and a third of everything they pick in a good year. In a wet August it can push past forty percent.

Those growers are not losing that fruit because it is bad. They are losing it because it is shaped wrong.

Our actual agreement

Since 2017 our tomato contracts have worked like this. We agree a price per pound before planting, not at harvest. We commit to a volume for the whole season rather than week by week. And we buy on flavour and ripeness rather than on grade, which in practice means we take a great deal of fruit that a wholesaler would refuse.

The price we pay sits about twenty percent above the wholesale rate for graded fruit. That is the number people find strange. We are paying more for produce that the conventional market values at less.

The reason is straightforward. Guaranteeing a grower that their cracked and lobed fruit has a home changes their entire planting decision. It means they can plant the varieties that actually taste like something rather than the ones that survive a pallet. Hominy Creek Farm went from four varieties to eleven over three seasons on the back of that agreement, and eight of those eleven would never clear a wholesale grade.

We are not paying twenty percent more for tomatoes. We are paying twenty percent more for the right to influence what gets planted.

What it means in the kitchen

Buying this way forces a different way of cooking, and this is the part I actually enjoy.

  • Fruit arrives at wildly different ripeness on the same day, so we sort at the door into three buckets: slice today, cook tomorrow, sauce by Thursday.
  • A cracked tomato has a short life. Anything with a break in the skin goes into service that day or into the pot that afternoon.
  • We cannot promise a specific variety on a specific night, which is why the menu says heirloom tomato and the server tells you which four are on the plate.
  • Nothing is refrigerated. Cold destroys the volatile compounds that make a good tomato smell like a tomato, and once they are gone they do not come back.

The toast on our menu is the simplest thing we make and the hardest to fake. Bread, cultured butter, sliced fruit, salt, torn basil. If the tomato is mediocre, there is nothing to hide behind. That is the point of putting it on the menu at all.

The honest downside

I am not going to pretend this is free of cost or risk.

  1. We waste more than a kitchen buying graded fruit. Soft and split tomatoes do not last, and even with a same-day plan we lose a few percent.
  2. The plate price has to carry the higher cost per pound, which is why it is sixteen dollars and not nine.
  3. When a season goes badly, it goes badly for us specifically. In 2019 a late blight took most of two farms in ten days and we had no tomato dish for three weeks. We wrote that on the chalkboard rather than buying in from Florida.
  4. It takes more labour. Sorting a mixed crate takes a cook fifteen minutes that a uniform case would not.

We accept all four because the alternative is a tomato plate that tastes like every other tomato plate. There are plenty of those, and none of them need our help.

So when you get the toast this month and one slice is deep purple with a green shoulder and another is orange and slightly misshapen, that is not an accident of the kitchen. That is the whole argument, sitting on a piece of bread.

Marisol Vandergriff

Marisol runs the kitchens across all three Farm Table Kraft restaurants and writes every menu by hand before it is typeset. She grew up in Madison County, North Carolina, and cooked in Charleston and Chicago before coming home in 2013.
