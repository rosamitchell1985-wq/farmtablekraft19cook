Buying the whole animal, and what that forces us to cook

By Desmond Achebe, co-owner and operations June 2, 2026 8 minute read

In the spring of 2015 a hog farmer in Marshall offered us a better price if we took whole animals instead of cases of pork chops. We said yes for the wrong reason. We said yes because it was cheaper. Ten years later it is the single decision that has shaped this company more than any other, and the savings turned out to be the least interesting part.

The arithmetic nobody explains

A finished hog at around 250 pounds live weight yields roughly 180 pounds of hanging carcass, and from that you get somewhere near 140 pounds of usable meat and fat once you have boned it out. Break it down properly and you are looking at something like forty-two distinct cuts and products, once you count the trim, the fat, the bones and the offal separately.

Here is the problem. Six of those cuts sell themselves. Loin chops, tenderloin, belly, shoulder for pulling, ribs, and to some extent the ham. A restaurant that buys cases buys exactly those six, because they are what guests order without needing persuasion.

When you buy the whole animal, you get those six and you also get everything else, in exactly the proportion the pig grew it. A single hog gives you maybe eight to ten loin chops. It also gives you two full shoulders, forty pounds of fat, a set of trotters, the jowls, the heart, the liver, the kidneys, the caul fat, the skin and a bucket of trim.

You cannot sell a hog by selling chops. There are not enough of them. This is the entire lesson.

What it forces onto a menu

Once the animal is in your walk-in, the menu stops being a wish list and becomes a plan for using what you have. That constraint has produced more good dishes here than any brainstorming session ever has.

  • Jowl becomes guanciale, cured for three weeks and then used in almost everything that needs a savoury backbone.
  • Fat gets rendered into lard, which is why our pastry shell tastes the way it does and why the duck fat potatoes have a second fat in the pan.
  • Trim becomes sausage, which becomes the sausage in the beans, which becomes a staff meal on Sunday.
  • Bones become stock, which becomes the base of the braise, which is the lamb shoulder dish on the current menu.
  • Skin gets scored, dried overnight and fried into a bar snack that outsells three cocktails.
  • Heart is trimmed, sliced thin, grilled hard over the coals and served with sorghum and chili. It is the best value dish in the building and about half the guests who order it have never eaten heart before.

None of that appears on a menu written by someone with a distributor catalogue. It appears when you have forty pounds of fat and a problem.

The real costs

I want to be specific, because whole-animal buying gets romanticised and the romance leaves out the invoice.

  1. Labour. Breaking a hog properly takes a trained butcher between ninety minutes and two hours. At our wage that is roughly forty dollars of labour per animal, every week, forever. A case of chops takes zero minutes.
  2. Storage. An animal takes up walk-in space in a way that cases do not, and it takes it up all at once. We had to add a second cabinet in 2016 purely to handle carcasses and dry-ageing at the same time.
  3. Skill dependency. If your butcher leaves, you have a serious problem. We now cross-train every line cook on breakdown for exactly this reason, which costs more training hours.
  4. Menu inflexibility. If the heart dish does not sell this week, we still have hearts next week. You cannot simply stop ordering the parts that move slowly.

Against that, the savings are real but smaller than people assume. We pay roughly $4.10 per pound hanging weight, which works out around $5.30 per pound of usable meat once you account for the bone and the loss. Buying the same weight as individual primal cuts would land closer to $7.40. So the raw saving is meaningful, and then labour eats a chunk of it back.

Where the money actually comes from

The margin does not come from the discount. It comes from what happens to the cheap parts.

A pork chop has a food cost of roughly thirty percent on our menu at thirty-nine dollars. That is a normal, respectable number and it is what most restaurants live on. The braised shoulder, made from a cut that costs us a fraction of what the loin does, runs closer to nineteen percent. The sausage in the bean dish is under fifteen. The stock made from bones we already own has a food cost that is essentially the price of onions and time.

So the six cuts that sell themselves pay the rent, and the thirty-six that do not are where the profit lives. That is the inversion nobody tells you about when they talk about nose-to-tail cooking as an ethical position. It is an ethical position, and it is also the reason we survived 2020.

What we throw away

Almost nothing, and I say that without pride because it took years to get there. Current waste from a broken hog is under three percent by weight, mostly connective tissue that will not render and a small amount of bone dust.

Getting to that number was not clever. It was a series of unglamorous decisions: buy a grinder, buy a sausage stuffer, build a curing cabinet, train everyone on stock, and accept that the walk-in will always contain a tray of something that needs using by Thursday.

If you eat here regularly, you have eaten most of an animal without particularly noticing. That is the goal. The dish should be good first and virtuous second, and if the only reason to order something is that it prevents waste, then we have not cooked it well enough yet.

Desmond Achebe

Desmond handles leases, payroll, purchasing and the produce truck for all three restaurants. He cooked on the line for six years before moving to the operations side, which he says is the only reason anyone in the kitchen listens to him.
