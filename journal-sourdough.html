Eleven years of the same sourdough starter

By Rebecca Tull, head baker May 9, 2026 9 minute read

The culture that raises every loaf in all three of our restaurants was started in a plastic quart container in October 2016 with stone-ground rye flour and water from the tap on Biltmore Avenue. It has been fed twice a day, every day, ever since. It has never been dried, never been frozen deliberately, and never been replaced.

People ask whether that matters, or whether it is a story we tell. The honest answer is that it matters less than bakers claim and more than sceptics assume, and the interesting part is in the detail.

What a starter actually is

A sourdough starter is a stable community of wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria living in a flour and water paste. The yeasts produce carbon dioxide, which is what lifts the loaf. The bacteria produce lactic and acetic acid, which is what gives the bread its flavour, its keeping quality and its slightly tacky crumb.

That community reaches an equilibrium specific to how you feed it. Feed it more often at a warmer temperature and the lactic bacteria dominate, giving a milder, yoghurt-like sourness. Feed it less often and cooler and the acetic side rises, giving a sharper, more vinegary edge. Change the flour and the whole balance shifts, because you are changing the food supply.

So an eleven-year-old starter is not eleven years of accumulated magic. It is eleven years of consistent conditions producing a culture that is extremely well adapted to our specific flour, our specific room temperature and our specific schedule. That adaptation is the real asset. A new starter would take about three months to become as predictable, and predictability is what lets me bake 180 loaves a day without thinking hard about any single one.

The schedule

The working culture lives at around 78 degrees Fahrenheit in a covered tub near the proofing cabinet. It gets fed at 4:00 AM and 4:00 PM in a one to three to three ratio by weight: one part starter, three parts water, three parts flour.

The flour is a blend we settled on in 2019 and have not changed since:

  • Seventy percent white bread flour from a mill in Cleveland County, milled from soft red winter wheat.
  • Twenty percent whole rye, which is what the culture was started on and what it is happiest eating.
  • Ten percent whole wheat, stone ground, for the extra mineral content that keeps fermentation lively.

There is a second culture in a jar in the walk-in that gets fed once a week and exists purely as insurance. There is a third, dried into flakes and sealed in two mason jars, one of which lives in my kitchen at home. I will explain why in a moment.

How it changes across a year

The culture is not the same organism in January that it is in July, and any baker who tells you their process is fixed year round is not paying attention.

  1. Summer. The kitchen sits warm and the culture accelerates. Bulk fermentation drops from around five hours to three and a half. We shorten the final proof and bake earlier, and the bread runs slightly milder because the lactic side takes over.
  2. Autumn. The most stable period of the year. New crop flour comes in around October with higher enzyme activity, which makes the dough slacker for a few weeks until we adjust the hydration down by two percent.
  3. Winter. Everything slows. Bulk stretches to six or seven hours, so the overnight retard gets shorter to compensate. The bread is noticeably sharper in February than in June, and regulars notice.
  4. Spring. The hardest season to read, because the room temperature swings ten degrees between a cold morning and a warm afternoon. This is when we rely most on watching the dough rather than the clock.

The week in 2021

In March 2021 the compressor on the main walk-in failed on a Saturday night and nobody noticed until Sunday morning. The box had climbed to 61 degrees. Most of what was in there was fine or recoverable. The backup starter jar, which had been fed four days earlier and was already well past its peak, was not.

When I opened it, the smell was wrong. Sharp in a way that read acetone rather than vinegar, with a grey liquid layer sitting on top and a pink cast at the edge where it met the glass. Pink or orange discolouration in a starter is the one sign that means you throw it out rather than try to rescue it, because it can indicate bacterial contamination that has nothing to do with a healthy culture.

The working culture in the bakery was untouched, because it lived at room temperature and had been fed at 4:00 AM as usual. We lost the backup and nothing else. But it was the closest we have come, and it changed our practice permanently.

Since then there are always three: the working culture, a fed backup in a different refrigeration unit, and dried flakes stored off site. Drying is straightforward. Spread the starter thin on parchment, let it dry at room temperature for two days until it is brittle, break it into flakes and seal it. It will rehydrate after several years.

What it gives the bread

A well-adapted culture gives you three things: reliable rise, a crumb that stays soft for two days rather than one, and a flavour with actual structure to it rather than a flat sour note.

Our loaf goes into service as the sourdough and butter plate, becomes the base of the tomato toast in summer, gets torn under the whole roast chicken, and turns up as rye crisps beside the trout rillettes when we bake the rye version on Thursdays. One culture, five jobs.

The starter is not a mascot. It is a piece of equipment that happens to be alive, and it needs the same discipline as anything else in a kitchen: the same feed, at the same time, every day, whether or not anyone is watching.

Rebecca Tull

Rebecca has run the bakery since 2018 and owns the starter in every sense that matters. She sets the flour blend, bakes for all three restaurants out of the Asheville kitchen, and starts work at 3:40 in the morning six days a week.
